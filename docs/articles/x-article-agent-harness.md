# Our QA team doesn't exist

Over the past two months we've built Brainstorm, a local-first knowledge OS: an Electron shell hosting some twenty sandboxed apps, from notes and databases to a calendar, a mail client, and a full web browser, all of it encrypted and living on your own disk. Most of the code is written by AI agents, which is the part people ask about, though it's the least interesting part of the story I want to tell here.

The interesting part is what happened when we noticed that the agents were lying to us. Not deliberately, and not even technically, since every claim they made was true in the narrow sense: the tests passed, the types checked, the build was green. But anyone who has shipped software knows there is a canyon between "the tests pass" and "the thing works," and an agent that writes the code, then writes the tests for that code, then runs those tests and reports success, is a student grading its own exam with the answer key it wrote the night before. The app would come back from these sessions supposedly finished and then fall apart the moment a human touched it in a way no test had anticipated, which is to say the moment a human touched it at all.

We tried the obvious remedies first, stricter review passes and coverage floors and a rule that every bug had to be reproduced in a failing test before anyone was allowed to fix it. All of that helped, and all of it missed the point, because the problem was never that the tests were too few. The problem was that tests can only check what a developer thought to worry about, and the things that actually ruin software live precisely in the territory nobody thought to worry about. What we needed wasn't more assertions. What we needed was a user.

So we invented one.

## The woman who runs her whole company in our app

Mira Anand is the solo founder of a one-person research and advisory studio called Northbound. She sells a paid newsletter, takes advisory clients on the side, and runs the entire operation inside Brainstorm: the client CRM lives in our database app, the editorial calendar next to it, the research and the drafts in notes, the weekly cadence in tasks and a journal, the reading list in bookmarks, the strategy sprawled across a whiteboard. She is opinionated about tools in the way that solo founders are, quick to notice friction and disinclined to forgive it.

She is also, of course, not a person. She's a persona played by an agent and driven through Playwright, and if that were the whole trick it wouldn't be worth writing about. What makes her useful is that her workspace is real, and by the standards of a two-month-old product it is old. Her vault is a persistent encrypted database that has been accumulating actual work nearly as long as the app has existed, with all the sediment that implies: imported files, renamed projects, half-finished drafts, records created by versions of the code that no longer exist. When a session runs, it boots the production build against that lived-in vault, Mira sits down to do a day of founder work, and the harness quietly captures screenshots and console output the entire time. Afterwards the captures get read back, and every single thing that got in her way, every dead button and clumsy flow and impossible task, becomes a numbered entry in a friction log.

The whole arrangement rests on one rule, and it's the rule I'd defend with my life at this point: Mira never opens the repo. She has no access to the code and no ability to route around a problem by patching it, which means that when the interface can't do something, her only recourse is to write down that it can't, in her own voice, with a screenshot. What I was trying to do, what happened, what I expected instead. The clumsiness is the deliverable. On the far side of that wall sits the engineering role, which triages every entry, reproduces the bug in a failing test before touching anything, ships the fix, and answers in a shared chat channel; and when an entry is really a missing feature wearing a bug's clothing, the two roles argue it out in a war room document first, the engineer asking what Mira actually needs and Mira answering from the reality of her business, until they converge on something worth building. Friction flows one way across the wall and fixes flow back, and neither side is permitted to do the other's job.

## Then she started hiring

Here's where it got fun. A founder poking at an app will only ever find founder problems, and after a few weeks of Mira we could feel the blind spots hardening. So Northbound grew. Mira wrote a role brief in the notes app, stood up a candidates pipeline in the database, scheduled interviews on the calendar, linked scorecards back to each candidate's row, and hired her first employee, which meant we ran an entire hiring funnel through the product just to give it someone new to disappoint.

Marcus, the designer, came aboard skeptical, because he's the kind of designer who resents being asked to learn a new tool and says so. He is exacting, a little impatient, allergic to inconsistency, and his craft trial was literally a design review of Brainstorm itself. After him came Priya, a research editor who lives in citations and cross-links and embeds, and who will hunt down the one reference that doesn't resolve or the search query that misses a document she knows she filed. Then Dana, an operations hire who refuses on principle to do anything twice and files a complaint the moment the software makes her repeat herself. And finally Sol, an interaction engineer who thinks in states and keystrokes, who tabs through every surface and notices the one-pixel shift on press, the missing focus ring, the button that looks perfectly clickable and does nothing at all.

Five people who don't exist, each with a lens the others lack, each finding a class of defect the others walk straight past.

## What the log actually catches

Some recent entries, chosen because no unit test on earth would have flagged them. Calendar events named "ipeline ready," because the title field races the event creation and quietly eats your first keystrokes. An imported note whose name appears correctly in the window header, the sidebar, and the database view, while the editor itself shows a gray "Title" placeholder, three surfaces telling the truth and one lying. Nested bullet lists in an AI-generated answer collapsing into a single dash-riddled line of prose. And my favorite: posting to X silently failing because our browser's own tracking protection had blocked the site's scripts, and although a one-click "trust this site" escape hatch existed in a menu, nothing at the point of breakage breathed a word about it. That last one wasn't even a bug in the strict sense, since everything behaved exactly as designed, which is exactly why only a frustrated user could have surfaced it.

The log recently passed entry number 426, across more than four hundred recorded sessions, and the vault those sessions run against has become the most valuable test asset we own. Fresh seed data is clean, and clean data lies to you; bugs prefer history. The empty-title bug above only manifests in notes imported before a particular fix landed, a condition no freshly seeded fixture would ever contain. We learned the value of that sediment the hard way, when we wiped the vault once to get pristine marketing screenshots and vaporized the whole business in the process, the CRM, the candidates, weeks of accumulated state, and then spent a long penitent stretch of sessions rebuilding it. There is now a standing rule, with backups, that the vault is permanent. You do not delete your best test fixture, and your best test fixture is somebody's messy desk.

## The part worth stealing

So the loop runs on: a persona works a real day in the app, writes down what hurt, the engineer reproduces it in a failing test and ships the fix and replies in a channel you can tail live while the sessions grind along. When collaboration features landed we booted two complete instances of the app with separate cryptographic identities over an encrypted relay and had Mira share a document with Marcus, for real, and revoke his access when he rolled off the project, also for real.

The product ships because agents write the code. It's becoming good because a different set of agents, with different jobs and a wall between them and the repo, have to live inside what got shipped. If you're building anything with agents, that's the piece I'd steal: don't ask the agent that built the thing whether the thing works. Give some other agent a job that depends on it, take away its ability to fix anything, and make written frustration the only voice it has.
