# Our server can't read your notes. That's the boring part.

Brainstorm is a local-first knowledge OS — an Electron shell hosting some twenty sandboxed apps, all of it encrypted and living on your own disk — and when people ask about the security model they usually ask the question everyone knows to ask, which is whether the company can read your stuff. The answer is no, and I'll explain how, but I want to be honest that this is the least interesting part of the story. End-to-end encryption is table stakes now. Any product that syncs your data and can't say the server is blind is simply behind, and being not-behind is not a thing worth writing an article about.

The interesting part is the button that says *Remove access*, and everything we had to admit to ourselves before we could build it honestly.

## The lie in the revoke button

Here is a thing that is true and that most software will never say to your face: you cannot un-share a secret. Once a person has read your document, they have read it. It is in their eyes, on their disk, in a screenshot they took, in the memory of a machine you do not own. When you click *Remove access* in almost any collaboration tool, the interface performs a small theatre of control — the row disappears, the avatar greys out — and lets you believe you reached into their computer and pulled the knowledge back out. You did not. Nobody can. The bell does not un-ring.

We decided early that we would not perform that theatre. Brainstorm's revocation is forward-only, and we say so in the actual dialog, in plain words, at the moment you remove someone: *they will keep access to anything they could already see; they will not be able to read changes from now on.* This sentence cost us arguments. It is less reassuring than the lie. But a security model that reassures you about a thing it cannot deliver is worse than no model at all, because it spends your trust on a fiction and leaves you exposed exactly where you feel safest. So the entire design is organized around a guarantee we can actually keep — *from now on* — rather than one we can't.

Getting *from now on* to be cryptographically real, and not just another row disappearing from a list, turned out to be most of the work.

## Membership is data, not a key

The usual way to encrypt a shared workspace is to give the workspace one key. Everybody in the space holds it, everything in the space is sealed under it, and it works beautifully right up until someone leaves. Now that key is burned — the person walking out the door still has it — so to make their departure mean anything you have to mint a new key and re-encrypt every document in the space and hand the new key to everyone who stayed. Kick one contractor off one project and you've triggered a re-encryption of ten thousand documents they mostly never touched. This is the tar pit that swallowed a generation of local-first tools, and you can feel it in their products: sharing is heavy, revoking is heavier, and so both are quietly discouraged.

Brainstorm doesn't have a workspace key. Every single entity — every note, every database row, every whiteboard — carries its own key, its own DEK, and the shared secret is never handed around as a secret. Instead, for each person who has access, that one key is *wrapped*: sealed under that person's public key and stored next to the entity like any other piece of metadata. Adding a collaborator is not a redistribution of secrets across the whole space; it is one new wrap, and they can immediately read the whole history. Removing one is the same idea run backward: mint a fresh key for that one entity, wrap it for everyone who remains, and from that moment new edits are sealed under a key the departed person simply never receives. One entity rotates. Nothing else in your vault so much as flinches. Membership, in other words, is data — a signed little record living at the same layer as the content — and not a fragile shared secret you have to rebuild from scratch every time the group changes.

The relay that ferries all these encrypted updates between devices sees the shape of the traffic and nothing else: ciphertext blobs, routing tokens, signatures it can check to throw away forgeries cheaply. Not your names, not your titles, not your links. It is built to be worthless to whoever steals it, which is the only property a server you don't control should ever be trusted to have.

## Everything is guilty until proven innocent

Underneath the collaboration story sits a blunter one about the apps themselves. The user is the trust root. Everything else — every installed app, and this is the part that matters, *including our own code* — is treated as untrusted until a check says otherwise. Each app runs in its own operating-system process with no Node, no filesystem paths, no network, seeing only opaque file handles and a narrow bridge to the shell. It gets exactly the capabilities you granted it, named and scoped and recorded, checked on every single call. And the check fails closed: if the ledger that records what you allowed is unreadable for any reason, the answer is not "probably fine," the answer is *no*. A bug in our own permission code should cause an app to lose power, never to silently gain it. Better a button that's greyed out than a door that's quietly open.

Embedded content gets an even tighter cage. A block dropped inside a document renders in a sandboxed frame bound to exactly one entity, with zero capabilities of its own, so the worst a malicious one can do is lie about the single thing it was asked to draw. It cannot reach sideways into the note next to it. The blast radius is one frame, by construction.

## The morning Marcus could still read the file

If you read the last thing I wrote, you met Mira Anand, the fictional founder who runs her whole company inside Brainstorm and files a bug the instant the software wrongs her, and Marcus, the prickly designer she hired. They are personas played by agents, walled off from the repo, and their entire job is to live inside what we ship and write down what hurts. Which is how we found out our revoke button was, for a while, exactly the theatre I promised you we'd avoided.

Marcus rolled off a project. Mira opened the share panel and removed him, the row vanished, the avatar greyed out — the whole reassuring pantomime. And on Marcus's machine, in another booted instance of the app with its own cryptographic identity, the document kept updating. Every edit Mira made after she "removed" him decrypted cleanly on his screen, because we had shipped the *policy* of revocation — a signed record saying *revoked* — without the *cryptography* of it. The key never rotated. His device already held it. Nothing about a row disappearing from a list reaches across the network and changes what a key can open. The friction log caught the one thing no unit test of ours had thought to assert, which was whether a removed person, on a real second machine, was actually removed.

And then the fix nearly committed the opposite sin, which is the part I find genuinely funny. When we built the real rotation — mint the new key, re-wrap it for everyone who stayed — an early version quietly declined to install the new key on devices that already held the old one, on the reasonable-sounding logic that they already had *a* key for this entity. The tests were green. But that shortcut meant the survivors — the people who were supposed to keep working — would have been handed a rotation that locked *them* out while the whole point of the exercise, the person we were trying to remove, was unaffected in the moments that mattered. We had written a revoke button that removed the wrong people. It took modelling the receiving side, not just the sending side, to see it — asserting that the collaborator who stays can still open the document tomorrow, which is a thing you only think to check once a fake designer has made you distrust your own green checkmarks.

## The part worth stealing

So the honest shape of the thing is this. The server can't read your data, which is the price of admission and not a headline. Sharing is one wrap and revoking is one rotation, because keys hang off entities instead of pooling in a workspace. Your apps, and our code, get power only by explicit grant and lose it the instant anything is unsure. And when you take access away, the product tells you the truth about what that does — new edits, sealed away from them forever; old ones, already in their eyes, gone from your reach the way spoken words are.

If you're designing anything that guards other people's information, that last move is the one I'd steal: find the thing your security genuinely cannot promise, resist every instinct to paper over it, and build the architecture and the wording around the promise you *can* keep. The strongest sentence in a security model is usually the one admitting a limit. Ours took a designer who doesn't exist, reading a file he shouldn't have been able to, to make us write it down.
